Higher Education

Resources for higher education.

This page collects resources for undergrad and graduate students navigating higher education — scholarships, research opportunities, reading on academia, and professional development. Some of this reflects my own path through astrophysics; I've tried to make it broadly useful even if you're not in astronomy. This is a living list.

1. Scholarships

Undergraduate

  • Barry Goldwater Scholarship — The premier undergraduate award in science, math, and engineering in the US. For sophomores and juniors planning research careers; up to $7,500/year. Nominated by your institution.
  • Rhodes Scholarship — Full funding for graduate study at the University of Oxford. Open to US citizens 18–24; 32 scholarships awarded annually. Highly competitive.
  • Marshall Scholarship — Funds graduate study in the UK for up to three years. Open to US citizens; typically applied for in senior year of undergrad.
  • Truman Scholarship — $30,000 for graduate study in public service fields. For juniors committed to careers in government, policy, education, or public service. Nominated by your institution.
  • NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program (GRFP) — While primarily a graduate fellowship, seniors in their final year of undergrad are also eligible to apply. $37,000/year stipend + tuition allowance for three years. See Graduate section below.
  • Udall Scholarship — For students committed to careers related to the environment, or Native American students in any field. $7,000 award. Sophomores and juniors; nominated by institution.
  • APS Scholarships for Minority Undergraduate Physics Majors — The American Physical Society offers scholarships for underrepresented minority students in physics, supporting both tuition and research.
  • Society of Women Engineers (SWE) Scholarships — SWE administers scholarships for women and underrepresented genders in engineering and related STEM fields at the undergrad and grad levels.
  • QuestBridge College Prep Scholars — For high school juniors, but worth knowing: QuestBridge-affiliated college scholars programs continue supporting students through undergrad.

Graduate

  • NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program (GRFP) — The most widely known US graduate fellowship in STEM. $37,000 annual stipend plus $12,000 cost-of-education allowance for three years. Apply in fall of your first or second year of grad school (or senior year of undergrad). Apply early — deadlines vary by field.
  • Hertz Fellowship — One of the most prestigious and competitive STEM fellowships. Five years of support (~$38,000/year stipend) plus tuition. For PhD students in applied physical, biological, and engineering sciences.
  • DOE Computational Science Graduate Fellowship (CSGF) — Four-year fellowship for graduate students in computational science. $45,000/year stipend; requires a practicum at a DOE national lab. Strong fit for astrophysics, materials science, climate, and related fields.
  • NDSEG Fellowship (National Defense Science and Engineering Graduate) — Three years of support for graduate students in STEM who are US citizens. Run by the Department of Defense.
  • Ford Foundation Fellowships — For PhD students and postdocs who are members of groups historically underrepresented in higher education. Pre-doctoral, dissertation, and postdoctoral awards available.
  • Paul & Daisy Soros Fellowship for New Americans — $90,000 over two years for immigrants and children of immigrants pursuing graduate study in any field. Highly competitive and very selective.
  • AAS Fellowship Programs — The American Astronomical Society maintains information on fellowships relevant to astronomy and astrophysics graduate students. Check the AAS website's "Grants & Prizes" section.
  • NASA Fellowships (FINESST and others) — NASA's Future Investigators in NASA Earth and Space Science and Technology (FINESST) program funds graduate student proposals in heliophysics, astrophysics, planetary science, and Earth science.

2. Articles & Reading on Higher Education

Navigating college and graduate school is much easier when you've read about other people's experiences — especially if you're first-gen or don't have family members who went through these systems. Below are some pointers; I'll add specific links as I compile my favorites.

  • How to apply to graduate school in astronomy / physics — There are several widely-shared blog posts and guides on applying to PhD programs in physics and astronomy. Look for guides by current professors or grad students — they tend to be more candid than official department websites.
  • AstroBetter — A wiki and blog maintained by professional astronomers with practical advice on applying to grad school, using astronomy software, conference culture, job applications, and much more.
  • Prepare for the Roads — First-gen college student navigation resources.
  • "So You Want to Go to Grad School" (various authors) — Search for this phrase — there are several excellent essays by academics at various career stages about what a PhD is, what it isn't, and how to decide if it's right for you.
  • Matt Might's blog on PhD life — Matt Might (CS professor) has several widely-read posts including "The Illustrated Guide to a Ph.D." and advice on productivity and grad school culture.
  • The Illustrated Guide to a Ph.D. (Matt Might) — A famous short visual essay on what a PhD actually is. Worth five minutes of your time before you decide to pursue one.
  • Books: "The Ph.D. Grind" by Philip Guo — A candid memoir of a computer science PhD. Available free online. Even if you're not in CS, the honest account of grad school dynamics is valuable.
  • First-generation student experience essays — Add links here to essays or blog posts about first-gen college and grad school experiences that resonated with you personally.
  • Inside Higher Ed — News and opinion on higher education in the US. Good for staying informed about trends in academia, graduate education policy, and the job market.

3. Research Resources

Finding Undergraduate Research Opportunities

  • NSF Research Experiences for Undergraduates (REUs) — NSF-funded summer research programs at universities and labs across the country. Paid ($500–$600/week typically), with housing often provided. The NSF site has a searchable database of all active REU sites. In astronomy, many major observatories and universities host REUs.
  • Pathways to Science: Undergraduate Opportunities — Searchable database of undergraduate research, internship, and fellowship opportunities, with filters for field and background.
  • AAS List of Astronomy REU Sites — The American Astronomical Society maintains a list of astronomy-specific REU programs, including international opportunities.
  • Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) Summer Program — Paid summer research at the home institution of Hubble and James Webb Space Telescope science operations.
  • Cold-emailing professors for research — One of the best ways to find research is to email faculty directly. Keep your email short: introduce yourself, say specifically which paper or project you're interested in, and ask if they have space for an undergraduate researcher. Personalization matters — professors can tell when an email is a template.

Astronomy-Specific Research Tools

  • NASA ADS (Astrophysics Data System) — The primary search engine for astronomy and astrophysics research papers. Covers refereed journals, conference proceedings, and preprints. Free access to most papers.
  • arXiv: astro-ph — Free preprint server where most astronomy papers are posted before (or simultaneously with) journal publication. astro-ph.GA = galaxies, astro-ph.SR = stars, astro-ph.CO = cosmology, etc.
  • Astropy — The standard Python library for astronomy. If you're doing any computational astronomy research, you'll use this. Excellent documentation and tutorials.
  • HST/JWST Instrument Documentation (STScI) — If you work with Hubble or JWST data, STScI maintains all instrument handbooks and data reduction guides. Free online.

How to Read a Research Paper

  • "How to Read a Paper" by S. Keshav — A short (3-page) guide to reading research papers efficiently using the "three-pass" method. Widely recommended in STEM graduate programs.
  • Advice from your advisor or group — Honestly, the best way to learn to read papers is to read them with someone more experienced and ask questions. Your research advisor or group members are the best resource here.

4. Other Resources: Mentorship, Professional Societies & Diversity Programs

Professional Societies

  • American Astronomical Society (AAS) — The primary professional society for astronomers in the US. Hosts two major conferences per year (AAS and HEAD, DESI, etc.) and maintains resources for students including the AAS job register and prize fellowships.
  • American Physical Society (APS) — Professional society for physicists. Runs division-specific conferences, publishes Physical Review journals, and has programs for student members including travel grants.
  • SACNAS (Society for Advancement of Chicanos/Hispanics and Native Americans in Science) — Hosts the largest diversity conference in STEM — the NDiSTEM conference — and provides mentorship, funding, and community for underrepresented scientists at all career stages.
  • NOBCChE (National Organization for the Professional Advancement of Black Chemists and Chemical Engineers) — Annual conference, scholarships, and professional network for Black scientists and engineers.
  • Society of Women Engineers (SWE) — Professional society for women and underrepresented genders in engineering and STEM. Student sections at many universities; annual conference.
  • AISES (American Indian Science and Engineering Society) — Professional society and conference for Indigenous scientists and engineers. Scholarships, mentorship, and student chapters.

Mentorship & Diversity Programs

  • AAS Astronomy Ambassadors — Training and community for early-career astronomers interested in education and public outreach.
  • AURA Diversity Programs — AURA (which operates NSF's optical observatories and STScI) runs internship programs aimed at increasing diversity in astronomy.
  • Vera Rubin Observatory / LSST Early Career Resources — If you're interested in survey astronomy, Rubin Observatory has resources and community for early-career scientists.
  • inSPIRE (or similar mentorship networks) — There are several informal mentorship networks in astronomy for first-gen and underrepresented students; ask in your department or at an AAS meeting.

General Advice

  • Talk to graduate students, not just faculty. — Graduate students will give you a more honest picture of what day-to-day research life is like in a group or department. When visiting a potential PhD program, always request time to talk to current students without faculty present.
  • Apply broadly to grad school. — Admissions to PhD programs in physical sciences are unpredictable. Apply to at least 8–12 programs spanning a realistic range. Application fees are often waived for low-income or first-gen applicants — ask.
  • Your GRE may not matter. — Many astronomy and physics PhD programs have dropped the physics GRE requirement. Check each program's current policy; don't let test prep eat your senior year if it's not required.
  • Advice on reaching out to potential advisors — Cold-emailing a potential PhD advisor is normal and expected. Be specific about their research and why you're interested. A short, personalized email beats a long generic one.
